# Researching Product Market

Most dropshipping stores fail on arithmetic, not on taste. Research exists to
kill bad products cheaply, before ad spend does it expensively.

## Run the numbers first

A product that cannot clear this does not need further research:

```
Contribution margin = price − COGS − shipping − transaction fees
Break-even CPA      = contribution margin
```

If a realistic cost per purchase on paid social is not comfortably **below**
the contribution margin, the product loses money at scale no matter how good
the creative is.

Rules of thumb worth respecting:

- Price under €25 rarely survives paid acquisition — margin is too thin
- Heavy or fragile items get eaten by shipping and breakage returns
- Long shipping times drive chargebacks; factor a refund rate, don't assume zero

State your assumptions explicitly when presenting these numbers. Do not present
an estimate as a measurement.

## Demand signals that actually mean something

| Signal | Reading |
|---|---|
| Ads running unchanged for months | Someone is profitable. Strongest signal available. |
| Rising search trend | Real demand, not just a fad spike |
| Many sellers, differentiated pages | Viable market, needs a sharper angle |
| Many sellers, identical pages | Saturated, racing to the bottom on price |
| Huge spike, no history | Likely a fad already peaking |

**Ads that have run a long time are the most reliable signal.** Nobody keeps
paying for ads that lose money. Meta's Ad Library is public and free.

Since 2026 the Ad Library exposes an **impressions filter (≥100k) on every ad**,
not just political ones. Stack four signals: ≥100k impressions, running ≥14 days
unpaused, 5+ creative variants from one advertiser, and a landing page with a
real offer. That combination is the strongest free evidence available.

## The repeat-purchase trap

Unit-economics calculators ship with repeat rates borrowed from real brands —
1.4 repeat orders in year one is a common template default. **For single-product
dropshipping to cold paid traffic, repeat purchase is near zero.** Use 0.0–0.2,
then sanity-check the "orders per customer" the tool reports back: if it says
2.1, the model is describing a brand you do not have.

This one input decides everything. With the template default, three candidates
that actually lose money all read as "healthy DTC economics."

**Judge dropshipping on the first order alone:** contribution margin minus CAC.
If that number is not clearly positive, the product is dead no matter how good
the lifetime story sounds.

## The offer is the lever, not the product

When first-order margin is thin, swapping product rarely fixes it — changing the
offer does. Raising AOV with a bundle or accessory spreads a nearly fixed CAC
over more revenue.

Worked example from this project: the same dog bowl at €32.90 single leaves
€4.00 per order after CAC. As a 2-pack at €49.90, with CAC rising only €18→€20,
it leaves **€12.03** — three times the margin, same product, same supplier.
Test the offer before abandoning a candidate.

## Where to look, legally

- **Meta Ad Library** — public, searchable, shows how long ads have run
- **Google Trends** — seasonality and trajectory
- **TikTok Creative Center** — public ad and trend data
- **Marketplace listings, read manually** — reviews are the richest source of
  customer vocabulary and objections
- **Official APIs** where a platform offers one

**Do not scrape AliExpress, Amazon or competitor stores.** It violates their
terms, gets IPs blocked, and automated collection of personal data (reviewer
names, profiles) triggers GDPR obligations. Manual reading is legal and, for
the number of products actually worth evaluating, fast enough.

## Reading competitors

Extract the argument, never the assets:

- Which objection do they answer first? That's their expensive lesson.
- How is the offer framed — bundle, subscription, free shipping threshold?
- What proof do they lean on?
- What do their negative reviews complain about? That is your differentiator.

Copying their copy or images is copyright infringement. Copying their
*structure* is just competent research.

## Red flags that end the evaluation

- **Branded, patented or trademarked** — counterfeits get stores seized and
  bank accounts frozen. Check before, not after.
- **Restricted categories** — medical devices, supplements, weapons, vape,
  anything with health claims. Ad platforms reject these and payment
  processors drop the account.
- **Electrical goods without certification** — CE/UKCA/FCC compliance is the
  seller's legal responsibility, including for dropshipped goods
- **Batteries or liquids** — shipping restrictions that surface after the first order

## Output of a research pass

For each candidate: unit economics with assumptions stated, demand evidence
with dates and links, 2–3 named competitors and what they do well, the main
objection to overcome, and a clear **go / no-go with the reason**.

An honest no-go is the most valuable output this skill produces.
